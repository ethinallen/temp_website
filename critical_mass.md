# Critical Mass


In July of 2024, I moved to downtown Princeton, NJ. Within two weeks, I was around town handing out fliers and letting people know that a global, community bike ride would be happening in the home to one of the most prestigous institutions in the world. During the past two years I have turned up on the last Friday of every month (when I could) and rode bikes with members of my community. These were bike riders of all ages, abilities, and backgrounds. During this time, my focus was simple: make this opportunity to safely bike around downtown Princeton known to all. I was completely okay with people not going, so long as that was their choice. Now, as I ride shotgun on a U-Haul bound for Seattle, I feel the need to finally share specific details around what motivated me to print fliers to begin with, how I learned about Critical Mass, how and why I talk about the ride, and why I think it is important for Princeton to have a recurrent community bike ride like Critical Mass.

To understand why I found myself designing, printing, and disseminating fliers, it's probably best to understand a little bit more about me and how I found myself riding a bike in Princeton to begin with.

I have, for the most part, always had bikes in my life in some capacity. In 6th grade, I did something that nobody else at my school had done: I aksed if I could ride my bike from school at the end of the day. It was a small, private school of about 300 students from pre-k to 8th grade. This was unusual but so was I. The allure of being able to get myself from school to a destination of my choosing (public library, 1300m away if we're being generous).

As I set off, backpack bungeed to my pannier rack, I was immediately hooked. No gas, no license, and no limit on where I could go beyond imagination and the limitations set forth by my parents. A few years later, I got a road bike. I fell further in love with bikes. Now 14, I didn't even need to rely on my parents to drive me to downtown first. I eagerly awaited getting my license but, in the interim, I found much joy in the pedalling of backroads out in Aiken County in South Carolina. The interactions were mostly respectful. The only time I remember feeling a little perturbed by my treatment on the road was when I was passed in the same lane as a motorcycle that was going so fast I honestly do not think that I heard it coming. Otherwise, I found myself mostly obsessed with my newfound freedom and was willing to confront what I thought was a required concession: When I am on a bike, I am tertiary on the road, at best. I don't think I thought much about this at first but my mind has often thought back to this moment as one that inspired curiosity and skepticism about our current approach to safety and protecting our most vulnerable road users.

As I got more into running, I stepped away from cycling. I was told it would hurt my aspirations more than it stood to help so I stopped with the day-long trips from my house in the county into downtown and traded that time in for many wonderful miles in and around [Hitchcock Woods](https://www.hitchcockwoods.org/).
